# Interiorization

## Definition

**Interiorization** names a historical movement in which questions once organized substantially through public conduct, ritual, communal judgment, institutional discipline, or external observance become increasingly examined as matters of inner motive, sincerity, intention, temptation, conscience, and self-understanding.

The proposed movement is:

~~~text
external observance / public conduct
        +
questions of motive and intention
        ↓
more explicit inward examination
        ↓
inner states become objects of judgment
~~~

## Problem pressure

A history of introspection needs a mechanism more precise than saying that people simply "became more inward."

Interiorization asks **what kinds of questions migrated inward**.

Examples include:

- whether an outwardly good act arose from pride;
- whether belief was sincere;
- whether temptation had already become inward consent;
- whether one's motives matched one's declared principles;
- whether identity should be understood through private experience as well as public role.

## Historical scope

This is an interpretive framework, not a claim that earlier or non-European people lacked inner life.

The relevant thesis is narrower:

> Particular institutions, genres, and religious practices can increase the social demand, vocabulary, storage, and procedural resolution devoted to examining inward states.

Interiorization can therefore occur in many times and cultures.

## Relation to interior surveillance

Once inner motive becomes morally or spiritually consequential, recurrent observation of that motive becomes useful.

Thus interiorization can enable [Interior surveillance](./interior-surveillance.md).

## Relation to self-legibility

Interiorization creates pressure for distinctions among:

- motive;
- desire;
- intention;
- temptation;
- sincerity;
- self-deception;
- emotion;
- identity.

Those distinctions can increase [Legibility of the self](./legibility-of-the-self.md).

## Boundaries

Interiorization is not:

- the invention of consciousness;
- proof of greater psychological depth;
- necessarily liberation;
- necessarily repression;
- synonymous with individualism;
- a one-directional historical law.

External ritual and inward examination can coexist.

## Relations

It is associated with the **early-modern inward turn**, can enable **interior surveillance**, and contributes to **making the self available to itself**.
