# customary-rights orientation

## Definition

A **customary-rights orientation** evaluates political and economic arrangements through claims grounded in established local practice, reciprocal obligation, inherited usage, and concrete entitlement.

Examples include:

- land use;
- water access;
- commons;
- grazing;
- household rights;
- customary rents;
- ritual access;
- expected elite obligations.

The orientation asks less:

> “What system would be ideal in the abstract?”

and more:

> “What are we legitimately entitled to because this social world has long been organized around these obligations?”

## Problem pressure

The Village side needed a moral vocabulary of its own.

Without it, Village politics could be misread as mere resistance to change or dependence on elite ideology.

Historical moral-economy cases show something more structured: communities often possess strong internal criteria for legitimate and illegitimate power rooted in customary guarantees.

## Core model

~~~text
repeated social practice
        +
shared memory
        +
reciprocal expectation
        ↓
customary entitlement
        ↓
violation perceived as illegitimate
        ↓
petition / bargaining / litigation / rebellion
~~~

## Claims and implications

Customary orientation is not blind traditionalism.

Customs can be renegotiated, selectively remembered, legally formalized, or strategically invoked.

The theory predicts that Village actors may tolerate ordinary inequality while customary obligations are honored, then mobilize sharply when those obligations are violated.

## Examples

### Land

A community defends long-used communal land against a new title regime.

### Water

Households resist a reform that ignores established allocation practices even if the reform is formally uniform.

### Elite obligation

A local hierarchy remains accepted while patrons honor expected subsistence protections, then loses legitimacy when those protections vanish.

## Non-examples

Any inherited practice is not automatically a customary right.

A custom imposed unilaterally by a dominant actor may lack reciprocal legitimacy.

Nor does the concept claim that customary rights are always morally superior to universal law.

## Boundaries and failure modes

Custom can preserve internal domination, exclusion, caste, gender hierarchy, or coercive obligation.

Therefore customary rights remain subject to anti-domination constraints.

Analysts should also avoid romanticizing “tradition” when evidence for the claimed custom is recent or contested.

## Operationalization / evidence

Evidence can come from:

- petitions;
- customary law;
- court testimony;
- communal minutes;
- land and water records;
- oral histories;
- repeated language of inherited entitlement;
- patterns of mobilization after specific customary violations.

The key is to identify a concrete expectation with social memory and reciprocal structure.
