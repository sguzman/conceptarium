# Genre lock

## Definition

**Genre lock** is a temporary state in which media appetite narrows strongly around one genre or aesthetic domain.

During a genre lock, works inside the active genre feel:

- immediately appetitive;
- emotionally congruent;
- easier to begin;
- easier to sustain attention toward;
- more rewarding even before detailed evaluation.

Works outside the genre may feel:

- inert;
- irritating;
- aesthetically wrong;
- cognitively expensive;
- impossible to care about;
- like substitutes for the thing actually wanted.

The defining feature is not merely preference.

It is **temporary admissibility narrowing**.

The mind behaves as if one genre currently satisfies the required representational or affective conditions for engagement.

## Problem pressure

The concept emerged from a period in which the user could barely tolerate playing anything that was not science fiction.

This was stronger than:

> “I like science fiction right now.”

The experience was closer to:

> “My brain rejects games that are not science fiction.”

The user described being “obsessed with brooding sci-fi” and later explicitly said:

> “I was genre locked to sci-fi.”

The active appetite included more than setting.

The desired package included:

- brooding atmosphere;
- dark environments;
- strong contrast;
- dramatic use of light;
- technological or urban futurity;
- bleakness containing small embers of hope.

This suggested that genre lock may operate through a cluster of aesthetic and emotional features rather than through a nominal genre label alone.

## Core model

The basic state is:

```text
current affective / cognitive appetite
        ↓
one representational family matches unusually well
        ↓
attention and desire concentrate there
        ↓
matching works become highly admissible
        ↓
nonmatching works lose motivational access
```

The genre acts as an **attention gate**.

This does not require conscious choice.

## Genre label versus latent feature bundle

Genre lock may not actually lock onto a taxonomy word such as “science fiction.”

The brain may be locking onto a feature bundle:

```text
brooding
+ technologically saturated
+ nocturnal
+ urban
+ high-contrast lighting
+ dark story
+ small hope signals
        ↓
experienced as "I need sci-fi"
```

This matters because some works formally outside science fiction may still satisfy the lock if they reproduce the relevant affective structure.

Conversely, a nominally science-fiction work may fail if it lacks the desired tone.

The genre label can therefore be an efficient conscious approximation of a deeper appetite vector.

## Admissibility narrowing

Genre lock is best understood as a change in the **entry threshold** for attention.

Normally, several genres may be acceptable.

Under genre lock:

```text
matching genre
   → low friction
   → immediate curiosity

nonmatching genre
   → high friction
   → “I don't want this”
```

This can make recommendations outside the active genre objectively good but subjectively unusable.

## Positive lock and negative exclusion

Genre lock has two sides.

### Positive concentration

The active genre becomes unusually rewarding.

### Negative exclusion

Alternative genres become unusually unrewarding.

A strong lock requires both.

If someone merely enjoys more science fiction than usual but can happily consume fantasy, comedy, or historical drama, that is heightened preference rather than a strong genre lock.

## Tone lock inside genre lock

A genre lock may contain a narrower **tone lock**.

For example, the active appetite may not be:

> science fiction

but:

> **aggressively brooding science fiction with strong visual contrast and small embers of hope.**

This explains why two games sharing a genre label can feel radically different in suitability.

Genre lock should therefore be modeled hierarchically:

```text
genre family
    ↓
subgenre
    ↓
tone
    ↓
visual / sonic texture
    ↓
specific current appetite
```

## Relation to closure pressure

Genre lock changes which unfinished objects become psychologically live.

An old unfinished game can remain dormant for months.

Then:

```text
current genre lock
       +
unfinished matching game
       ↓
match becomes salient
       ↓
closure pressure reactivates
       ↓
return impulse
```

This is especially powerful when the unfinished game already satisfied the lock strongly.

The game feels doubly privileged:

1. it is exactly the kind of thing currently wanted;
2. it is already open and unfinished.

## Selection efficiency

Genre lock can be useful.

It reduces search space.

When appetite is highly specific, trying to force broad variety wastes attention.

Genre lock can function as a temporary optimization:

> Stop asking what is generally good. Ask what fits the active channel.

This can make media selection easier and more satisfying.

## Constraint cost

The same narrowing can become frustrating when:

- the active genre has few suitable works;
- available works have technical problems;
- the person feels guilty for ignoring other media;
- the lock persists longer than desired;
- the desired feature bundle is more specific than the genre label suggests.

The person may repeatedly try excellent nonmatching works and bounce off them.

## Examples

### Brooding science-fiction lock

A player temporarily cannot sustain interest in fantasy, historical games, or ordinary contemporary settings.

Games with oppressive futurist architecture, neon darkness, industrial soundscapes, and bleak-but-not-hopeless stories become disproportionately attractive.

### Horror lock

A viewer may temporarily want only horror and find comedy or drama intolerably low-stakes.

### Mystery lock

A reader may enter a period where ordinary literary fiction feels directionless unless there is a mystery structure generating epistemic tension.

## Non-examples

Genre lock is not:

- having a favorite genre;
- a stable lifelong taste;
- genre expertise;
- deliberately choosing a themed month;
- simply disliking one game outside the preferred genre;
- social identity around a fandom.

The concept refers to a **temporary narrowing of motivational access**.

## Boundaries and failure modes

### Taxonomy overfitting

The conscious genre label may be too coarse.

The actual appetite may concern mood, pacing, setting, color, sound, narrative structure, or thematic texture.

### Identity confusion

A temporary lock should not automatically be treated as a permanent identity.

“I only want sci-fi right now” is different from “I am fundamentally a sci-fi person.”

### Recommendation mismatch

Recommendation systems may fail by respecting only the genre label while ignoring the active tone bundle.

### Lock reinforcement

Consuming only one genre may sometimes intensify the lock by further calibrating attention to that feature distribution.

This is a hypothesis, not yet established.

### Forced diversification

Trying to break the lock by consuming unrelated media may produce needless friction if the lock is temporary and harmless.

## Operationalization / evidence

Possible indicators include:

- repeated abandonment of otherwise appealing works outside one genre;
- unusually fast engagement with matching works;
- explicit frustration that “nothing else works right now”;
- strong narrowing of recommendation criteria;
- reactivation of old matching works;
- sustained appetite for several works sharing the same aesthetic bundle;
- low transfer of interest to nominally adjacent genres that lack the desired tone.

A useful diagnostic question is:

> **Is the person choosing this genre, or is this currently the only genre that feels available to desire?**

The latter is genre lock.

## Relations

### Closure pressure

Genre lock can reactivate closure pressure for unfinished works that match the current appetite.

Closure pressure, in turn, can make one matching unfinished work dominate the available choice set.

### Tone lock

Tone lock is a likely subordinate concept if it recurs independently: narrowing around mood, atmosphere, or affective texture even across genre boundaries.

It is preserved here but should not yet be promoted without further use.

### Media appetite

Genre lock should eventually sit inside a broader model of changing media appetite: how mood, fatigue, novelty, memory, aesthetic saturation, and current concerns alter what kinds of representations are motivationally accessible.

### Problem being solved

Why can a person with broad stable taste temporarily become unable to engage with otherwise good media outside one genre?

Ordinary preference language was too weak because the phenomenon involved **motivational exclusion**, not just ranking.

### Conceptual ancestors

Potential external neighbors to investigate later include:

- mood-congruent media selection;
- selective attention;
- state-dependent preference;
- aesthetic satiation;
- interest dynamics.

The local term should be preserved even if partial precedents exist because its emphasis on **temporary genre-level admissibility narrowing** is useful.

### Later refinements

The first canonical entry distinguishes:

- genre lock from favorite genre;
- genre label from latent feature bundle;
- positive concentration from negative exclusion;
- genre lock from tone lock;
- stable taste from temporary motivational narrowing.

## Open questions

- What triggers genre locks?
- Are they driven more by mood, cognitive fatigue, recent exposure, current intellectual preoccupation, or sensory appetite?
- How long do they typically persist?
- Does consuming the locked genre satisfy the appetite and dissolve the lock, or reinforce it?
- Can the latent feature bundle be inferred more accurately than the conscious genre label?
- How often does genre lock reactivate closure pressure toward unfinished matching works?
- Is “tone lock” a distinct cross-genre phenomenon?
- Do genre locks become stronger during periods of reduced attentional flexibility?
