# Contestability

## Definition

**Contestability** is the practical ability of people affected by a decision, classification, rule, or authority to challenge it in ways that can actually change outcomes.

A contestable system allows some combination of:

- inspection;
- explanation;
- objection;
- appeal;
- correction;
- alternative providers;
- counterevidence;
- collective organization;
- review by another authority;
- rule revision;
- exit.

The crucial word is **practical**.

A right to appeal that cannot realistically be used is decorative.

## Problem pressure

Contestability emerged as the most useful operational bridge from anti-domination to institutional design.

“Do not dominate people” is morally clear but architecturally incomplete.

A designer needs to ask what features make arbitrary power difficult.

Contestability supplies an answer:

> Build systems in which affected people can push back.

## Core model

```text
consequential decision
      ↓
visible reason / rule
      ↓
opportunity to challenge
      ↓
independent or alternative pathway
      ↓
possible correction
```

A system becomes dangerous when the chain stops at:

```text
decision → compliance
```

## Dimensions of contestability

### Epistemic

Can a person dispute the facts or data used?

### Procedural

Can they challenge whether the rule was followed?

### Normative

Can the rule itself be changed?

### Institutional

Is there another body capable of reviewing the first?

### Market/exit

Can the person route around the authority?

### Collective

Can affected people organize rather than contest individually?

## Relation to institutional legibility

Institutional legibility is asymmetrical if the institution can see and classify the person while the person cannot understand how the institution sees them.

A more anti-dominating architecture aims for some degree of **bidirectional legibility**:

- the institution can process the person;
- the person can inspect enough of the institutional process to challenge it.

This does not require exposing every internal detail.

It requires exposing the parts necessary for meaningful contest.

## AI application

Contestability is particularly important for AI-mediated decisions and assistants.

A trustworthy AI system should allow users to:

- correct assumptions;
- supply counterevidence;
- inspect sources where relevant;
- distinguish fact from inference;
- reject proposed goals;
- recover from misclassification;
- avoid silent substitution of the system's preferences.

This connects directly to **sovereign system builders**.

## Non-examples

Contestability is not:

- endless veto power;
- guaranteed success in appeal;
- the absence of rules;
- merely receiving an explanation;
- merely having a feedback form.

A challenge must have a plausible route to consequence.

## Boundaries and failure modes

### Procedural overload

Too many veto points can make systems incapable of acting.

### Expert capture

Appeals may require the same expertise controlled by the original institution.

### Cost asymmetry

A nominally open process can remain inaccessible through time, money, language, or psychological burden.

### Retaliation

A system is not truly contestable if challengers are punished for using the mechanism.

## Operationalization / evidence

Measure:

- appeal success rates;
- cost and time of challenge;
- availability of independent review;
- explanation quality;
- reversibility of decisions;
- provider alternatives;
- retaliation risk;
- collective representation;
- frequency with which user-supplied evidence changes outcomes.

## Open questions

- What is the minimum viable contestability for high-speed automated systems?
- How should contestability work when decisions are distributed across many institutions?
- Can contestability substitute for exit where monopoly infrastructure is unavoidable?
- How can challenge remain accessible without making institutions ungovernable?
