# Residual fiscal rights

## Definition

**Residual fiscal rights** are the claims a contributor retains after a compulsory levy has validly transferred some control over contributed value to the state.

The source bundle included:

- **provenance** — where did the value go?
- **attribution** — what did this contribution finance?
- **allocation** — which legitimate public uses may receive some share?
- **refusal** — which uses may be excluded where law permits?
- **audit** — can transformations be independently verified?
- **standing** — can the contributor personally enforce the rights?

The framework is modular. A political order might recognize provenance and audit while rejecting strong allocation or refusal rights.

## Problem pressure

Once the source accepted compulsory taxation for collective goods, “taxation is theft” no longer captured the interesting problem. The unresolved issue became what rights remain **after** lawful collection.

The word residual avoids the false binary between complete private ownership and complete fiscal disappearance.

## Core model

~~~text
pre-tax property rights
        ↓ lawful compulsory levy
rights transferred to state
        +
residual contributor rights
        ↓
provenance / attribution / allocation / refusal / audit / standing
~~~

## Claims and implications

The framework distinguishes:

1. May the state compel the transfer?
2. What rights survive once it does?

Answering yes to the first does not logically answer the second. Strong versions include allocation and refusal; weak versions may preserve only accounting and enforcement.

## Examples

### Clean example

A taxpayer can trace the contribution, inspect allocation rules, direct a legally discretionary share, exclude narrow prohibited uses, and sue when those rights are violated.

### Borderline example

A government provides individualized receipts and audit data but no allocation rights. Some residual rights exist, but the bundle is thin.

### Cross-domain example

A beneficiary may retain information and enforcement rights even while a trustee legitimately controls assets.

## Non-examples

A general democratic right to vote on budgets is not itself a contributor-specific residual fiscal right.

A politician’s promise to “listen to taxpayers” is not an enforceable right.

## Boundaries and failure modes

The framework can slide into plutocracy if contribution-weighted fiscal authority leaks into unrelated political authority. The source explicitly separated one-person-one-vote from contribution-linked allocation.

Conflict-of-interest rules are also essential; otherwise direct allocation can become self-dealing or disguised tax avoidance.

## Operationalization / evidence

For each proposed right, specify holder, scope, object, procedure, remedy, exception, and termination condition. A mature implementation must answer what concrete action a contributor can take when the right is violated.

## Relations
