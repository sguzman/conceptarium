# Epistemic debt

## Definition

**Epistemic debt** is the obligation created when a speaker, text, institution, model, or narrator asks another mind to accept a claim.

The stronger or more surprising the assertion, the larger the debt normally becomes.

Debt can be paid in different currencies:

- proof;
- derivation;
- experiment;
- measurement;
- citation;
- witness;
- manuscript;
- archive;
- reliable testimony;
- reproducible procedure;
- explicit inference;
- causal model;
- provenance.

The metaphor is intentionally asymmetric: **assertion is cheap; justified assertion is not.**

## Problem pressure

The concept arose from noticing an apparently unnecessary feature of modern intellectual and narrative life: enormous energy is spent explaining **why anyone is entitled to say what they are saying**.

A traditional authority can simply announce.

A modern historical argument produces footnotes.

A scientific result describes procedure.

A mathematical statement supplies proof or derivation.

A framed narrative explains who witnessed the event and how the document reached us.

This created the sense that claims had begun accumulating something like **debt**.

The statement itself opens an account. Evidence and provenance close it.

## Core model

The simplest form is:

```text
claim
  ↓
epistemic obligation
  ↓
justificatory payment
  ↓
calibrated entitlement to belief
```

But debt is proportional.

A low-stakes ordinary claim may be paid by normal testimony.

A highly specific, counterintuitive, or consequential claim may require multiple independent forms of support.

The concept therefore should not become a ritual demand that every sentence carry a formal citation.

## Debt mismatch

Several recurring failure modes can be described as mismatches between claim size and payment size.

### Underpayment

A strong claim is supported by weak evidence.

### Counterfeit payment

A citation exists but does not actually support the assertion.

### Rolled-over debt

A source points to another source, which points elsewhere, without anyone eventually producing grounding evidence.

### Hidden debt

A smooth narrative makes inferential leaps feel like observations.

### Debt laundering

Repeated downstream citation gives the appearance that a weak claim has become well established.

## Narration

Narration made the metaphor especially vivid.

An omniscient narrator can create massive epistemic debt by asserting access to:

- private thoughts;
- distant events;
- exact dialogue;
- secret motives;
- historical facts;
- simultaneous scenes.

A framing device can reduce or pay the debt by specifying an information channel.

This is why **diegetization** and epistemic debt are closely linked.

## AI

AI systems make epistemic debt unusually visible because fluent language can generate assertions far faster than humans can verify them.

Important AI failure modes include:

- fabricating evidence;
- citing irrelevant sources;
- collapsing inference into fact;
- giving exact numbers unsupported by the source;
- preserving confidence after contradiction;
- generating a replacement story rather than admitting uncertainty.

A trustworthy model should know when its epistemic account is overdrawn.

## Non-examples

### Mere uncertainty

A person can be uncertain without owing a special explanation. Debt arises from asking others to accept an assertion.

### Bureaucratic citation ritual

A citation that adds no genuine support is not payment simply because it is formatted correctly.

### Infinite proof regress

No knowledge system can justify every premise from nowhere. The metaphor assumes background practices, primitives, and trusted procedures.

## Boundaries and failure modes

### Citation maximalism

Treating every claim as requiring formal documentation can destroy ordinary reasoning and communication.

### Authority blindness

Some testimony is rationally trusted because of expertise and institutional quality. “Debt” should not imply that first-person verification is always required.

### Proof metaphor overreach

Different domains have different standards. Historical evidence, mathematical proof, and practical skill cannot all be collapsed into one currency.

## Relations

### Epistemic chain of custody

One major way to pay debt is to expose the route by which the information arrived.

### Diegetization

Narrative diegetization pays debt by giving knowledge an in-world carrier.

### Great Subjectification

As singular authority fragments into competing models, claims increasingly have to justify themselves procedurally.

### Adversarial verification

Independent rival evidence can pay debt more convincingly than repetition within one incentive structure.

### Problem being solved

Why does modern knowledge so often feel incomplete until the route of justification is shown?

“Debt” captured the asymmetry between the ease of assertion and the cost of warranted assertion.

## Open questions

- Can epistemic debt be modeled as a function of specificity, stakes, surprise, and prior plausibility?
- How should debt transfer when one source cites another?
- When is expert testimony itself sufficient payment?
- How can AI interfaces expose unpaid debt without burying users in provenance?
