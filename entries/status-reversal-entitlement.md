# Status-reversal entitlement

## Definition

**Status-reversal entitlement** is the failure mode in which justice is modeled as **reversing the direction of domination** rather than ending domination.

Its structure is:

~~~text
A dominated B
      ↓
A was wrong
      ↓
therefore justice requires
B may now dominate A
~~~

The concept is not about changing status positions in general. Restitution, political empowerment, loss of illegitimate privilege, and punishment can all reverse relative status without creating an entitlement to dominate.

The defining move is normative symmetry-breaking: prior victimhood becomes the reason a new domination relation is now morally licensed.

## Problem pressure

The unbounded-revanchism cluster needed a precise endpoint for claims that move beyond repair.

Without this distinction, any redistribution of power could be mislabeled “status reversal.” The concept therefore focuses specifically on a theory of justice where **the identity of the permitted dominator changes but the permission structure itself survives**.

## Core model

~~~text
old domination
      ↓ exposed as unjust
grievance
      ↓
grievance-to-jurisdiction conversion
      ↓
new asymmetrical permission
      ↓
status-reversal entitlement
~~~

Anti-domination instead aims for:

~~~text
old domination
      ↓
rights / constraints / counter-power
      ↓
no party receives standing title to dominate
~~~

## Claims and implications

Status reversal can be psychologically understandable and politically common without being normatively legitimate.

The concept does not require that former high-status actors retain old privileges. Losing unjust privilege is not being dominated.

The analytic burden is to show that the new arrangement creates **arbitrary or unanswerable power**, not merely that status has changed.

## Examples

### Clean example

A movement explicitly argues that because one group historically monopolized authority, the formerly subordinate group should now possess comparable unchecked authority over the former group as a matter of justice.

### Social example

A previously controlled sibling gains independence and then treats past subordination as permanent permission to control the former controller’s unrelated choices.

### Borderline example

An institution temporarily reserves leadership positions for a historically excluded group as a bounded remedial policy with review and sunset conditions. Status changes, but a standing domination entitlement is not necessarily created.

## Non-examples

Restitution, affirmative remedies, removal of illegitimate authority, representation, or equalization of opportunity are not status-reversal entitlement merely because they change relative status.

Punishing a proven wrongdoer under general law is also not equivalent to granting the victim general sovereignty.

## Boundaries and failure modes

The concept can easily become a rhetorical defense of entrenched privilege if every loss of dominance is called reverse domination.

Operational use must therefore focus on **actual discretionary power, asymmetrical rights, lack of appeal, and indefinite jurisdiction**, not status discomfort.

## Operationalization / evidence

Measure:

- whether rules differ by historical identity;
- whether asymmetry is remedial and bounded;
- whether powers have sunset or review conditions;
- whether affected parties retain basic contestability;
- whether the stated goal is equal standing or inversion;
- whether prior injury is invoked as permanent justification.

## Relations

Status-reversal entitlement is produced by **Grievance-to-jurisdiction conversion**, supports the architecture of **Unbounded revanchism**, and directly contrasts with **Anti-domination**, which seeks to remove arbitrary power rather than transfer it.
