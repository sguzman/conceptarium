# Village rebellion vs. revolution

## Definition

**Village rebellion vs. revolution** distinguishes two forms of political agency that earlier Fallen/Village formulations risked collapsing.

Village actors can independently:

- riot;
- resist;
- litigate;
- defend land;
- refuse extraction;
- organize locally;
- preserve customary claims;
- rebel.

A revolution requires additional work:

- trans-local universalization;
- portable political language;
- coalition across communities;
- strategic coordination;
- institutional blueprint;
- successor authority.

The distinction is therefore about **scale and authorship**, not agency versus passivity.

## Problem pressure

Historical testing directly falsified the crude idea that local communities simply follow elite ideologues.

Mexican and Andean cases showed communities using documents, courts, unions, memory, occupation, and local leadership.

But the correction did not eliminate organizational asymmetry. A land grievance does not automatically generate a national theory of property, citizenship, class, or state reconstruction.

The distinction preserves both facts.

## Core model

~~~text
local injury / customary violation
        ↓
local organization
        ↓
resistance / rebellion

REVOLUTION adds:
        +
brokerage across local worlds
        +
portable abstraction
        +
coalition
        +
successor institutions
~~~

## Claims and implications

Village rebellion can be highly sophisticated.

Revolutionary authorship is not morally superior; it is a different organizational function.

The framework predicts that Rising Fallen or status brokers may disproportionately perform the trans-local abstraction work, but this is probabilistic and empirically defeasible.

## Examples

### Village rebellion

Several communities mobilize to restore land and water rights without seeking to redesign the entire state.

### Revolutionary coalition

Local agrarian grievances are connected to a national program, military strategy, constitutional language, and successor institutions.

### Hybrid actor

A locally embedded figure can become a national political author without ceasing to carry Village commitments.

## Non-examples

A local protest is not “nonpolitical” merely because it lacks a universal ideology.

Conversely, elite ideological writing without local organization does not itself constitute a revolution.

## Boundaries and failure modes

The distinction can become elitist if “revolution” is treated as the only serious political agency.

It can also be too sharp: rebellions can spread horizontally, communities can author general principles, and revolutionary projects can emerge through distributed processes.

The term should therefore mark an analytical gradient rather than a caste division.

## Operationalization / evidence

Code separately:

- local grievance generation;
- local organization;
- trans-local coordination;
- abstraction into portable categories;
- doctrine/program production;
- successor institution building.

Then test whether the latter functions are disproportionately associated with different actors than the former.

## Relations

The distinction separates Village rebellion from **Political authorship**, requires **Ideological brokerage** for many trans-local transformations, is associated with **Organizational asymmetry**, and is grounded in the agency of **Embedded community**.
