# Credential-to-jurisdiction conversion

## Definition

**Credential-to-jurisdiction conversion** is the mechanism by which evidence of qualification in one role is treated as granting authority over people or questions beyond the warrant of that qualification.

The credential may be formal or informal:

- degree;
- license;
- professional membership;
- elite institutional affiliation;
- demonstrated technical success;
- moral prestige;
- revolutionary or activist credentials;
- recognized intellectual sophistication.

The key error is not respecting expertise. It is converting **epistemic or status evidence into political title without a separate authorization step**.

## Problem pressure

Infinite license required a concrete entry point.

The user’s motivating liberal-managerial example was not simply “experts exist.” It was the perception that credentials are metabolized into:

> “We have earned the right to manage you forever.”

Existing **Models don’t confer title** captured a related epistemic boundary, but credential-to-jurisdiction conversion names the social mechanism by which titles, degrees, affiliations, or success cross that boundary.

## Core model

~~~text
credential
   ↓
evidence of competence / status in domain X
   ↓
deference in X
   ↓ conversion
generalized authority over Y / persons
   ↓
Infinite license
~~~

## Claims and implications

Credentials can legitimately be prerequisites for authority. A medical license can justify clinical powers; a judicial appointment can justify adjudicative authority.

The conversion problem arises when the **scope of authority is inferred from the prestige of the credential rather than specified by a role, delegation, or constitutional rule**.

## Examples

### Clean example

A technically successful profession argues that its specialized expertise entitles it to make binding decisions on unrelated cultural or political questions without democratic authorization.

### Fallen example

A person interprets elite education and intellectual sophistication as evidence that less credentialed people are properly objects of management.

### Borderline example

A scientific advisory body strongly influences public policy during an epidemic. This may be legitimate domain-relevant authority; analysis should distinguish advice, delegated emergency power, and generalized political title.

## Non-examples

Requiring a professional license for a genuinely technical role is not credential-to-jurisdiction conversion.

Neither is choosing to defer voluntarily to someone because their expertise is relevant.

## Boundaries and failure modes

Anti-expert populism can misuse the concept to deny real competence. The correct question is **scope**, not whether credentials matter.

Formal authorization can also legitimately extend authority beyond narrow expertise—for example, an elected scientist serving as a legislator exercises political authority through election, not through scientific credentials alone.

## Operationalization / evidence

Identify:

- credential;
- domain it evidences;
- authority claimed;
- authorization mechanism;
- whether affected people can contest the authority;
- whether domain relevance is demonstrated;
- whether alternative expertise or lay interests can enter the process.

## Relations

Credential-to-jurisdiction conversion enables **Infinite license**. It contrasts with **Models don’t confer title** and **Prestige without sovereignty**, both of which preserve expertise or distinction while denying automatic generalized jurisdiction.
