# Fiscal non-extinguishment

## Definition

**Fiscal non-extinguishment** holds that lawful compulsory transfer is not itself sufficient reason to erase every remaining claim of the contributor.

The principle does not say taxation leaves full private ownership unchanged. A valid levy may transfer substantial rights to the state. The narrower claim is:

> only the rights actually transferred by the fiscal arrangement are transferred; collection is not a magical event that annihilates every other claim.

This creates room for continuing rights of accounting, attribution, provenance, bounded allocation, refusal, audit, or standing.

## Problem pressure

The source encountered a discontinuity:

~~~text
before collection:
private property / due process / individualized claims

after collection:
public revenue
~~~

If the transaction is compulsory, the intuition developed in the thread was that the recipient should face **more** fiduciary burden, not fewer obligations.

Without non-extinguishment, every downstream taxpayer right appears as a discretionary favor. With it, the burden reverses: one must explain which rights compulsion legitimately transfers and which remain.

## Core model

~~~text
compulsory levy
      ↓
specified rights transferred
      +
unspecified residual rights retained
      ↓
collection does not itself erase the remainder
~~~

## Claims and implications

This is primarily a normative principle, not an established doctrine of current tax law. It is compatible with compulsory taxation, general funds, redistribution, and broad legislative budgeting.

Its target is the assumption that collection alone settles every question of contributor standing and attribution. The principle may generalize beyond taxes: where consent is weak, institutions may owe heightened explanation, accounting, appeal, and fiduciary duties.

## Examples

### Clean example

A constitution permits compulsory taxation while guaranteeing every contributor an auditable provenance record and personal standing to challenge prohibited uses.

### Borderline example

A state provides aggregate spending transparency but denies contributor-specific claims. It partially respects accountability while rejecting strong non-extinguishment.

### Cross-domain example

A trustee controls assets for a defined purpose while beneficiaries retain accounting and enforcement rights.

## Non-examples

Refusing to recognize taxation as lawful at all is not fiscal non-extinguishment; the concept begins after assuming some compulsory levy is valid.

Claiming every taxpayer retains unilateral ownership of every paid dollar is stronger than the principle requires.

## Boundaries and failure modes

The residual-right bundle must be specified. If every conceivable preference becomes a retained right, the concept collapses into total taxpayer veto.

Non-extinguishment also cannot guarantee a unique mapping from every tax dollar to every expenditure where borrowing, money creation, or temporal pooling complicate attribution.

## Operationalization / evidence

A legal implementation would enumerate which rights survive collection, define enforcement, identify legitimate exceptions, and prevent ordinary budget practice from silently canceling them.

A conceptual test is simple: ask whether a claimed taxpayer right disappears **solely because collection occurred**. If yes, the system is relying on extinguishment and needs an independent justification.
