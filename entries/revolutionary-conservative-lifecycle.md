# revolutionary/conservative lifecycle

## Definition

The **revolutionary/conservative lifecycle** is a positional mechanism in which actors who attack an existing status order while excluded can become defenders of a new order after successful incorporation.

The recovered sequence is:

~~~text
exclusion
  ↓
counter-elite formation
  ↓
innovation / universalization
  ↓
Village mobilization or alliance
  ↓
victory
  ↓
institutionalization
  ↓
qualification / preservation / closure
~~~

## Problem pressure

The Fallen/Village theory repeatedly encountered a familiar historical puzzle: revolutionaries often become administrators, gatekeepers, or conservatives.

Calling this mere hypocrisy is sometimes accurate but incomplete.

The positional hypothesis is that actors experience different coordination and status problems before and after incorporation. The excluded actor sees closure from outside; the incumbent bears the costs of selection, stability, scarcity, and institutional survival.

## Core model

~~~text
Rising Fallen position
        ↓
attack on inherited closure
        ↓
successful incorporation
        ↓
Established Fallen position
        ↓
new order must select / reproduce / defend itself
        ↓
conservative or closure pressure
~~~

## Claims and implications

The lifecycle is a hypothesis, not a universal law.

Some revolutionaries remain revisionary after ascent. Some incumbents remain committed to opening. Some ideological commitments are stable despite status change.

The model predicts **positional pressure**, not deterministic moral reversal.

## Examples

### Revolutionary cadre

An insurgent movement attacks hereditary exclusion, wins power, then develops professional standards and gatekeeping for the institutions it created.

### Field insurgent

An outsider criticizes credential closure, establishes a new discipline, then defends standards for entry once the discipline becomes valuable.

### Political order

A movement universalizes rights while excluded, then later emphasizes competence and stability after becoming responsible for governing.

## Non-examples

Any person who becomes older or more conservative is not an example.

A principled shift caused by new evidence is not reducible to positional incorporation.

Nor is institutionalization inherently betrayal.

## Boundaries and failure modes

The model risks cynically reducing all moral language to status position.

That strong form is explicitly rejected. Actors can sincerely believe both opening and standards; salience can change with institutional role.

The empirical test must therefore examine whether rhetoric and policy systematically shift with incorporation while controlling for other causes.

## Operationalization / evidence

Track actors or cohorts across:

- exclusion;
- mobilization;
- entry into office/status;
- institutional responsibility;
- rhetoric about openness, standards, hierarchy, and legitimacy;
- actual gatekeeping behavior.

Strong evidence requires within-actor or within-cohort change linked to positional transition.

## Relations

The lifecycle begins in a **Rising Fallen** position and can produce **Established Fallen**. It often uses **Ideological brokerage**, is associated with **Moral universalization under exclusion**, and can culminate in **Ladder closure** or other forms of institutional preservation.
