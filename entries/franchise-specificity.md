# Franchise specificity

## Definition

**Franchise specificity** measures how much information the franchise name adds beyond a broad genre label. A highly specific franchise sharply narrows expectations about world, form, mood, narrative logic, or aesthetic treatment. A low-specificity franchise may still be famous and prestigious, but its name excludes little that the genre itself would not already exclude.

## Problem pressure

The question “why call this *Silent Hill*?” was fundamentally a specificity question. If the label increasingly means only “serious psychological horror,” then the franchise may remain prestigious while becoming less specific than it once was.

The immediate research pressure came from studying *Silent Hill*: if a rights-holder can move setting, ontology, narrative grammar, and aesthetic assumptions while retaining the same franchise label, what—other than authorization—makes the new work an instance of the same thing?

## Core model

```text
information supplied by franchise label
        minus
information already supplied by genre label
        =
franchise specificity
```

## Claims and implications

Specificity and quality are independent. A brilliant work can lower specificity by expanding the franchise into territory previously belonging to the genre at large. Strong branding can mask this loss because recognition remains high.

The concept is analytic rather than automatically evaluative. A change can be artistically excellent while still changing the franchise category.

## Examples

### Clean example

If hearing a franchise name lets an audience predict a distinctive setting logic, type of ambiguity, visual grammar, and thematic treatment that “horror game” alone would not predict, specificity is high.

### Borderline example

A sequel changes major surface features but preserves enough of the older franchise grammar that reasonable observers disagree about whether the category itself has changed.

### Cross-domain example

The same structure can appear in long-running film, comic, toy, software, restaurant, or institutional brands when a durable name outlives the narrower properties it once denoted.

## Non-examples

High visual polish, popularity, or a famous logo does not demonstrate specificity.

## Boundaries and failure modes

Specificity is not automatically good. Some franchises are intentionally broad anthologies. The concept measures categorical distinctiveness, not artistic merit.

Do not infer incompatibility merely from novelty. A strong identity can be elastic, and a historically unusual installment can still reveal a latent possibility rather than violate the franchise.

## Operationalization / evidence

Useful evidence includes comparison across installments, creator statements, audience categorization, recurring settings and structures, marketing language, changes in what features are treated as optional, and counterfactual tests such as: **if the franchise name were removed, what properties would still identify this work as belonging to the lineage?**

## Relations

Franchise personality **supports** specificity. Semantic dilution and identity collapse **threaten** it. Prestige without specificity names the important case where reputation survives after specificity weakens.
