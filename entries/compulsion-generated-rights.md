# Compulsion-generated rights

## Definition

**Compulsion-generated rights** is the broader principle that institutional coercion can itself generate additional duties toward the coerced party.

~~~text
less voluntary transaction
        ↓
less exit
        ↓
greater institutional burden of justification,
accounting, appeal, and remedy
~~~

rather than:

~~~text
less voluntary transaction
        ↓
fewer residual claims
~~~

## Problem pressure

The fiscal discussion began with the intuition that voluntary exchange contains a protection: exit. If the state removes that protection by compelling transfer, it is perverse to use the compulsion itself as the reason normal accountability disappears.

This principle generalized the tax argument into a broader anti-domination rule.

## Core model

~~~text
institution removes exit / refusal
          ↓
dependency on institution rises
          ↓
risk of arbitrary power rises
          ↓
countervailing rights should strengthen
~~~

## Claims and implications

This is normative, not a statement that every legal system recognizes such rights. It does not say all coercion is illegitimate. It says legitimate coercion may require more procedural architecture precisely because consent is weaker.

## Examples

### Clean example

Compulsory taxation is paired with heightened accounting and personal standing because the payer cannot choose another counterparty.

### Borderline example

Mandatory licensing includes appeal and review but no exit.

### Cross-domain example

A monopoly utility with no practical exit owes stronger procedural protections than a competitive discretionary service.

## Non-examples

A voluntary purchase does not normally generate the same level of public-law accountability merely because the buyer later regrets it. The principle does not imply unlimited veto.

## Boundaries and failure modes

If every inconvenience is called coercion, the principle expands without limit. Generated rights should be proportionate to dependency and institutional risk.

## Operationalization / evidence

Identify constrained exit options, discretionary power, stakes of error, available appeals, transparency, audit, and remedies. Compare otherwise similar voluntary and compulsory relationships.
