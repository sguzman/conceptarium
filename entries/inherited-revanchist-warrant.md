# Inherited revanchist warrant

## Definition

An **Inherited revanchist warrant** is a special claim grounded in prior injury that is treated as transferable from original victims to later descendants, successor institutions, or successor identity groups **without an independently specified rule for scope and expiration**.

The concept does not deny that legal claims, material harms, debts, or institutional consequences can persist across generations.

Its target is a stronger proposition:

~~~text
ancestor was wronged
      ↓
descendant inherits not only consequence or remedy
      ↓
descendant inherits standing exceptional authority
      ↓
later members of opposing category inherit liability
~~~

The revanchist component appears when inherited identity itself becomes the continuing warrant.

## Problem pressure

The unbounded-revanchism discussion immediately raised a temporal problem: grievance can outlive the original parties.

Some intergenerational claims are straightforward. Property can remain stolen. Treaties can remain violated. Institutions can persist. Material effects can be inherited.

The missing term was for a different structure: **the injury itself becomes a transmissible title**, even when the theory does not clearly distinguish inherited consequence from inherited moral jurisdiction.

## Core model

~~~text
generation 1:
injury → grievance → special claim

generation 2+:
identity / descent / institutional succession
          ↓
claim inherited
          ↓
scope / target / expiration specified?
     ├─ yes → potentially bounded succession
     └─ no  → inherited revanchist warrant
~~~

## Claims and implications

Inheritance alone does not make a claim revanchist. A descendant can inherit property rights, legal causes of action, treaty rights, or material disadvantages.

The analytical question is what exactly transfers:

- **harm consequence**;
- **remedial right**;
- **memory duty**;
- **status claim**;
- **authority over descendants of the opposing side**.

Only the latter forms create the strongest risk of unbounded revanchism.

## Examples

### Clean example

A political doctrine treats descendants of an injured group as permanently entitled to asymmetrical authority over descendants of an opposing group even when individual conduct, current injury, and remedy are irrelevant.

### Bounded counterexample inside the same domain

A family inherits a legally traceable claim to land taken unlawfully. The claim ends when title or agreed compensation is restored.

### Borderline example

A community preserves ceremonial obligations of apology across generations. This may be inherited memory or reconciliation practice rather than revanchist warrant if no coercive special jurisdiction follows.

## Non-examples

Teaching historical trauma, maintaining memorials, preserving group identity, or acknowledging inherited disadvantage are not inherited revanchist warrant.

Neither is inherited legal standing where the law specifies the object and satisfaction condition.

## Boundaries and failure modes

The concept is especially vulnerable to abuse because intergenerational injustice is real.

Analysts must not treat “descendants are affected” as equivalent to “descendants inherit permanent jurisdiction,” nor should they assume that claims expire simply because original victims die.

## Operationalization / evidence

Identify:

- original injury;
- current claimant’s relation to it;
- present material consequences;
- present liable target;
- transferable remedy;
- expiration or satisfaction condition;
- whether authority depends on descent alone.

A case strengthens when inherited identity substitutes for a fresh account of harm, liability, and remedy.

## Relations

Inherited revanchist warrant can intensify **Unbounded revanchism** by extending the claim across generations. It is often associated with **Closureless grievance** and **Moralized grievance**, but neither memory nor moralization alone implies revanchism.
