# reciprocal morality

## Definition

**Reciprocal morality** is moral order grounded in durable relations of mutual obligation.

Its characteristic questions are:

- Who owes what to whom?
- What help has been given before?
- What obligations attach to land, kin, patronage, or community?
- What counts as fair treatment inside an ongoing relationship?
- Which entitlements have become legitimate through repeated practice?

It is moral because it evaluates conduct, not merely because reciprocity is strategically useful.

## Problem pressure

An early Fallen/Village formulation risked implying that morality was mostly authored by status-seeking elites.

Historical and ethnographic correction made that untenable.

Embedded communities possess moral economies of their own.

Reciprocal morality names that locally generated normative order and prevents Village actors from being reduced to passive recipients of ideology.

## Core model

~~~text
repeated relationship
      +
memory / obligation / mutual aid
      ↓
reciprocal expectations
      ↓
local fairness judgments
      ↓
customary entitlement / sanction / solidarity
~~~

## Claims and implications

Reciprocal morality can coexist with hierarchy.

A patron may be accepted while expected obligations are fulfilled, then condemned when those obligations are violated.

This helps explain why Village populations can tolerate ordinary imperfection yet mobilize sharply when a moral threshold is crossed.

## Examples

### Mutual aid

A household receives labor because it previously contributed to others and is expected to reciprocate later.

### Customary protection

A local elite loses legitimacy after abandoning long-standing subsistence obligations.

### Commons

Access is judged through remembered contribution, need, and established reciprocal rules.

## Non-examples

Any exchange of equal value is not reciprocal morality.

A one-shot contract can be fair without depending on relational memory.

Nor is reciprocal morality automatically altruistic or universal.

## Boundaries and failure modes

Reciprocal morality can be highly particularistic.

It can exclude outsiders, enforce oppressive obligations, or preserve inherited hierarchy.

Anti-domination therefore remains an external constraint on local moral orders.

## Operationalization / evidence

Evidence can include:

- language of mutual obligation;
- customary entitlements;
- remembered contributions;
- sanctions for nonreciprocity;
- local fairness norms;
- mutual aid;
- threshold reactions when expected obligations are violated.

Ethnography, petitions, customary law, and communal records are especially useful.
