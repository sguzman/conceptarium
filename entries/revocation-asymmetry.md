# Revocation asymmetry

## Definition

**Revocation asymmetry** is a failure mode in which the evidentiary threshold for **gaining authority** is substantially lower or clearer than the threshold for **losing authority**.

Examples of positive warrant:

- credential earned;
- election won;
- crisis solved;
- institution founded;
- expertise demonstrated;
- moral sacrifice recognized.

If none of the corresponding negative evidence—failure, obsolescence, contradiction, changed conditions, revoked consent—can meaningfully reduce authority, legitimacy behaves like a one-way ratchet.

## Problem pressure

The Infinite license discussion needed to explain why a bounded warrant can become permanent even when its original basis weakens.

One answer is temporal laundering. Another is structural: the institution knows how authority is acquired but has no symmetrical logic for revocation.

This mirrors Conceptarium’s emphasis on corrigibility. A system that can absorb evidence only in the direction of more authority is not genuinely corrigible.

## Core model

~~~text
positive evidence
   ↓
authority increases

negative evidence
   ↓
authority unchanged

repeat
   ↓
one-way accumulation of jurisdiction
~~~

## Claims and implications

Revocation need not be perfectly symmetric with authorization. Removing authority can appropriately require stronger evidence because institutional stability matters.

The failure mode appears when revocation is so weak that the original warrant becomes practically irrebuttable.

## Examples

### Clean example

A professional body gains broad regulatory authority after demonstrated expertise, but repeated predictive failure, misconduct, and public rejection have no formal route to narrow that authority.

### Political example

An institution’s historic democratic mandate is continually cited as sufficient legitimacy while no current process allows affected people to withdraw delegated powers.

### Borderline example

A tenured office protects incumbents from ordinary political pressure but allows removal for defined misconduct. This may be deliberate stability rather than pathological asymmetry.

## Non-examples

High thresholds for removal are not automatically revocation asymmetry if they are explicit, usable, and proportionate to the need for independence.

Likewise, losing an argument need not strip someone of professional authority.

## Boundaries and failure modes

A simplistic symmetry rule would make institutions unstable. The right comparison is not “same threshold in both directions,” but whether **negative evidence has a real channel into authority state**.

Informal prestige is also difficult to revoke because no central actor grants it. The concept should distinguish institutional authority from distributed reputation.
