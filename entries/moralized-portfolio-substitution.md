# Moralized portfolio substitution

## Definition

**Moralized portfolio substitution** is substitutive allocation justified partly by a moral judgment about the incumbent aesthetic category. The existing type is not merely less profitable; it is treated as overrepresented, unhealthy, stereotypical, exclusionary, or otherwise normatively excessive.

## Problem pressure

The term was needed to distinguish simple diversification from a portfolio policy that intentionally reduces one category because its current share is itself considered a problem.

The immediate problem was how to describe finite character slots, marketing attention, art budgets, and audience targeting without pretending that every form of “inclusion” is purely additive.

## Core model

```text
incumbent aesthetic category judged excessive / harmful
        ↓
finite slots or attention reallocated
        ↓
alternative types gain share
incumbent type loses share
```

## Claims and implications

The mechanism can produce genuine opportunity costs for audiences attached to the reduced category even if the overall portfolio becomes more diverse.

These concepts describe allocation under scarcity. They do not assume that reallocations are illegitimate; the purpose is to make tradeoffs and constituency effects explicit.

## Examples

### Clean example

A studio deliberately cuts the number of conventionally glamorous female leads because it believes the old concentration represented unhealthy or exclusionary beauty standards.

### Borderline example

A sequel expands its cast and changes design priorities simultaneously, so some groups gain new representation while others lose relative share without losing absolute quantity.

### Cross-domain example

The same logic can apply to casting, publishing lists, advertising inventories, programming schedules, product portfolios, and institutional attention.

## Non-examples

Adding several new body types while leaving existing glamorous character production unchanged is additive diversity, not portfolio substitution.

## Boundaries and failure modes

A decline in one category does not prove moralized substitution unless there is evidence that its reduction was deliberate and normatively motivated.

Do not treat all cultural attention as strictly zero-sum. Budgets, roster sizes, and markets can expand. The relevant question is which resources are actually scarce in the decision being analyzed.

## Operationalization / evidence

Identify the scarce resource, the before/after allocation, the constituencies being targeted, and whether change is additive, substitutive, or mixed. Where possible, separate absolute quantity from portfolio share.
