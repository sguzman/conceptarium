# Boundary of the search space

## Definition

The **boundary of the search space** is the implicit limit on the psychological possibilities a person seriously considers when interpreting themselves.

A self-question such as:

> “What do I want?”

does not search every logically imaginable answer.

It searches a bounded space of answers that currently feel:

- conceivable;
- relevant;
- permissible enough to examine;
- compatible enough with the self to be considered;
- available in the person's conceptual vocabulary.

The boundary determines which hypotheses are generated at all.

## Problem pressure

Self-analysis can appear rigorous while remaining trapped inside a narrow hypothesis space.

~~~text
careful reasoning
      +
restricted candidate set
      ↓
confident answer
that never considered the real alternative
~~~

This means better introspection is not only about evaluating hypotheses more accurately.

Sometimes the prior problem is **generating a larger set of possible hypotheses**.

## Search versus evaluation

The distinction is:

~~~text
SEARCH
What possibilities are even considered?

EVALUATION
Which considered possibility best fits the evidence?
~~~

A person can be excellent at evaluation and still fail because the relevant possibility lies outside the search boundary.

## What shapes the boundary

The boundary can be shaped by:

- culture;
- family;
- morality;
- gender expectations;
- language;
- role identity;
- prior experience;
- shame;
- fear;
- available examples;
- imagination;
- conceptual categories.

The boundary is therefore both personal and socially scaffolded.

## Opening as boundary change

An **opening** can move the boundary.

~~~text
formerly impossible hypothesis
        ↓
becomes conceivable
        ↓
enters candidate set
        ↓
can now be tested, rejected, refined, or integrated
~~~

The important gain is not that the new hypothesis must be accepted.

It is that it can finally compete.

## Relation to admissibility

The **admissible self-model** concerns what a person permits to count as belonging to the self.

The search-space boundary is slightly earlier.

A possibility can be:

1. outside the search space entirely;
2. considered but deemed inadmissible;
3. admitted provisionally;
4. integrated durably.

This staging prevents “I rejected it” from being confused with “I genuinely considered it.”

## Diminishing returns

Once a major possibility enters the search space, repeating the same opening mechanism cannot make that exact possibility newly conceivable again.

That is one source of **diminishing returns of opening**.

## Examples

A person interpreting stress considers tiredness, workload, and anger but never considers fear because fear is outside their ordinary self-conception.

Someone considering future relationships searches only familiar role structures until exposure to a different model makes another possibility thinkable.

A person evaluating career dissatisfaction considers changing employers but never considers leaving the profession until a major disruption expands the search space.

## Non-examples

The boundary of the search space is not:

- a literal neurological boundary;
- the set of all thoughts a person could physically have;
- the same as moral permission;
- proof that excluded hypotheses are true;
- ordinary uncertainty among already-considered options.

## Boundaries and failure modes

### Infinite possibility problem

A search space cannot include every imaginable interpretation.

Boundaries are necessary.

### Expansion equals truth

Adding hypotheses improves coverage but can also add noise.

### Retrospective arrogance

After an opening, old omissions can look obviously irrational even when the missing category was genuinely unavailable.

### Social determinism

Cultural influence shapes the search space without fully determining it.

## Operationalization

A boundary becomes visible through:

- hypotheses repeatedly omitted despite relevant evidence;
- sudden appearance of new candidate interpretations after exposure to a concept or example;
- asymmetric possibility generation for self versus others;
- retrospective statements such as “I did not know that was an option”;
- durable changes in what future self-questions can consider.

## Relations

**Psychological closure** constrains the boundary.

**Opening** changes it.

The **admissible self-model** determines which considered possibilities can become legitimate self-descriptions.

**Diminishing returns of opening** emerges partly because a boundary, once crossed, has already changed the search space.
