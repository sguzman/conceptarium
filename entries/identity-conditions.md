# Identity conditions

## Definition

**Identity conditions** are the properties that make franchise membership intelligible from inside the work rather than solely from legal ownership or marketing. They need not be identical across installments, and they may be disjunctive or structural rather than a simple checklist.

## Problem pressure

The discussion repeatedly returned to a membership question: if corporate authorization is insufficient as an artistic explanation, what must a new work preserve so that attaching the franchise name communicates something non-arbitrary?

The immediate research pressure came from studying *Silent Hill*: if a rights-holder can move setting, ontology, narrative grammar, and aesthetic assumptions while retaining the same franchise label, what—other than authorization—makes the new work an instance of the same thing?

## Core model

```text
candidate work
     ↓
compare with franchise invariants / grammar
     ↓
meets sufficient identity conditions?
     ├── yes → internally intelligible membership
     └── no  → membership rests mainly on external authorization
```

## Claims and implications

Identity conditions make franchise arguments testable. Instead of debating whether a work “counts” in the abstract, participants can disagree about which conditions are necessary, sufficient, historical, or newly established.

The concept is analytic rather than automatically evaluative. A change can be artistically excellent while still changing the franchise category.

## Examples

### Clean example

A franchise may permit new protagonists and countries while still requiring a characteristic supernatural ontology, narrative relation between place and psyche, and specific mode of ambiguity.

### Borderline example

A sequel changes major surface features but preserves enough of the older franchise grammar that reasonable observers disagree about whether the category itself has changed.

### Cross-domain example

The same structure can appear in long-running film, comic, toy, software, restaurant, or institutional brands when a durable name outlives the narrower properties it once denoted.

## Non-examples

“The publisher owns the trademark” is a legal condition of authorized use, not an internal identity condition.

## Boundaries and failure modes

There may be no single necessary feature shared by every legitimate installment. Conditions can form a family-resemblance structure, and late entries can legitimately revise them. The concept should expose the revision rather than pretend no revision occurred.

Do not infer incompatibility merely from novelty. A strong identity can be elastic, and a historically unusual installment can still reveal a latent possibility rather than violate the franchise.

## Operationalization / evidence

Useful evidence includes comparison across installments, creator statements, audience categorization, recurring settings and structures, marketing language, changes in what features are treated as optional, and counterfactual tests such as: **if the franchise name were removed, what properties would still identify this work as belonging to the lineage?**

## Relations

Franchise ontology **constrains** plausible identity conditions. Internal definition **uses** them to state what the franchise is from the inside. Semantic dilution threatens them by making more conditions optional.

A vocabulary was needed for separating legal canon, artistic continuity, brand recognition, category boundaries, and quality. Ordinary judgments such as “good sequel,” “different direction,” or “still canon” compressed these into one question.

## Open questions

- Should identity conditions be modeled as necessary/sufficient rules, weighted prototypes, or multiple admissible paths?
- Who gets evidentiary weight when creators, rights-holders, critics, and audiences disagree?
- Can an installment retrospectively reveal an identity condition that earlier audiences had not articulated?
