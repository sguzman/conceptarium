# Quality-legitimized semantic drift

## Definition

**Quality-legitimized semantic drift** is semantic change whose main legitimating mechanism is excellence. A work moves the franchise boundary; its quality then converts what might have been treated as incompatibility into acceptable evolution. Later works inherit the expanded boundary.

## Problem pressure

The concept was needed to explain why “but it is good” can function as an answer to “but is this still the same franchise?” even though those are logically separate questions. In practice, quality supplies legitimacy to category expansion.

The immediate case was franchise evolution around *Silent Hill*, especially the possibility that successful, acclaimed departures may change the category more effectively than obvious failures.

## Core model

```text
boundary departure
      +
artistic success
      ↓
criticism of fit loses force
      ↓
new interpretation admitted
      ↓
precedent accumulates
      ↓
franchise meaning shifts
```

## Claims and implications

Quality becomes a mechanism of semantic authority. This creates a ratchet-like tendency because acclaimed departures are preserved in canon and memory, whereas weak departures can be discarded.

This family deliberately separates two judgments: **is the work good?** and **what does admitting this work do to the meaning of the franchise?**

## Examples

### Clean example

A brilliant installment introduces a looser franchise ontology; because audiences and critics celebrate the work, later creators can treat that ontology as established precedent.

### Borderline example

A well-received installment introduces several new assumptions but also preserves much of the older grammar; the key dispute is whether the change is ordinary evolution or category drift.

### Cross-domain example

The same logic applies to institutions, political labels, genres, product categories, and intellectual traditions whose names persist while membership criteria change.

## Non-examples

A franchise changes definition because of a corporate mandate despite consistently poor reception. That is semantic drift, but not quality-legitimized drift.

## Boundaries and failure modes

Success does not prove causal legitimacy. Researchers should identify whether quality actually reduced resistance or whether the change would have occurred for independent reasons.

“Dilution” here is semantic, not automatically qualitative. A broader category can contain better works than the narrower one it replaced.

## Operationalization / evidence

Track changes in category extension across time: which works are admitted, which traits cease to be necessary, what audiences expect from the label, what creators imitate, and whether formerly exceptional features become normal. Separate quality measures from identity measures.

## Relations

The mechanism **specializes** semantic drift, is **enabled by** good dilution, and **produces** accumulated-precedent redefinition. Canonical adhesion can stabilize the change once introduced.

## Provenance

### First known appearance

### Immediate context

The discussion distinguished ordinary franchise failure from the more subtle case where good work makes a broader or altered franchise definition harder to resist.

### Problem being solved

“Decline,” “bad sequel,” and “selling out” were inadequate because they assume aesthetic failure. The needed vocabulary had to describe identity change that can occur through success.

### Conceptual ancestors

## Open questions

- Can commercial success legitimize drift independently of artistic quality, and should that receive a separate term?
- How much of quality-legitimation is retrospective rationalization?
- Does critical prestige have more boundary-changing power than mass popularity?
