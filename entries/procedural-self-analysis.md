# Procedural self-analysis

## Definition

**Procedural self-analysis** evaluates the method by which an internal conclusion was reached.

It asks:

> Was the evidence adequate?

> Were standards applied consistently?

> Did one internal claim receive a burden of proof that another would never face?

> Was the process trying to understand, or trying to punish?

The object of analysis shifts from the verdict to the procedure.

~~~text
internal claim
      ↓
investigation
      ↓
tribunal procedure
      ↓
verdict
      ↓
PROCEDURAL REVIEW
"Was this a fair way to know?"
~~~

## Problem pressure

Self-analysis can become severe while still describing itself as objective.

A person may say:

> “I am only following the evidence.”

But the actual procedure may include:

- selective evidence;
- impossible burdens of proof;
- asymmetrical skepticism;
- guilt by provenance;
- humiliation as a decision rule;
- immunity for favored identities;
- no path to appeal.

The procedure can therefore be defective even when the person sincerely values truth.

## Procedural dimensions

### Evidentiary symmetry

Would the same evidence be treated the same way if it supported a favored conclusion?

### Burden of proof

Which claim must prove itself, and why?

### Reversibility

What evidence could change the verdict?

### Role reversal

Would the standard seem fair if applied to another person?

### Provenance handling

Does the origin of a state inform judgment without automatically invalidating it?

### Proportionality

Does the conclusion exceed what the evidence supports?

### Humane constraint

Can an impulse be contained without being humiliated or erased?

## Procedure versus outcome

A fair procedure can still produce an incorrect verdict.

A biased procedure can occasionally produce a correct one.

This is why procedural self-analysis does not substitute for substantive evidence.

It adds a second axis:

~~~text
VERDICT QUALITY
Is the conclusion true or well supported?

PROCEDURAL QUALITY
Was the route to the conclusion fair, symmetric, revisable, and non-punitive?
~~~

## Relation to internal tribunal

The [Internal tribunal](./internal-tribunal.md) adjudicates inner claims.

Procedural self-analysis audits that tribunal.

The tribunal remains necessary.

The goal is not abolition of judgment but **constitutionalization of judgment**.

## Relation to investigating the investigator

[Investigating the investigator](./investigating-the-investigator.md) exposes possible bias in the reflective authority.

Procedural self-analysis generalizes that discovery into rules and checks that can survive beyond one moment.

## Relation to analytical cruelty

A cruel tribunal often leaves procedural traces:

- harsher burdens for disfavored parts;
- presumption of guilt;
- no right to explanation;
- humiliation treated as evidence;
- refusal of appeal.

Procedural self-analysis can therefore help **prevent analytical cruelty** without requiring comforting conclusions.

## Examples

A person asks whether they would accept the same evidence standard if the claim concerned a friend instead of themselves.

Someone distinguishes “this impulse needs containment” from “this impulse proves I am contemptible.”

A person revisits an identity judgment after noticing that contrary evidence was automatically reclassified as fake.

## Non-examples

Procedural self-analysis is not:

- refusing all judgment;
- unconditional self-acceptance;
- therapeutic reassurance;
- replacing truth with kindness;
- endless process review;
- courtroom formalism imposed literally on the mind.

## Boundaries and failure modes

### Procedural perfectionism

No introspective process can satisfy infinite review.

### Mercy capture

A procedure can become biased toward exoneration just as it can become biased toward condemnation.

### False symmetry

Different claims may legitimately require different evidence.

### Meta-prosecution

Procedural review itself can become another punitive layer.

## Operationalization

A reusable checklist can ask:

1. What claim is being judged?
2. What evidence was admitted?
3. What evidence was excluded?
4. Who bears the burden of proof?
5. What would reverse the verdict?
6. Is the standard symmetric across favored and disfavored claims?
7. Is provenance being confused with legitimacy?
8. Does the process contain punitive surplus?
9. Is there a path to revision?
