# Good dilution

## Definition

**Good dilution** is semantic dilution caused by works whose artistic quality is not in dispute. The work succeeds on its own terms and may deserve praise, yet its admission broadens or alters what the franchise name can mean. Because criticism of fit can be mistaken for criticism of quality, the change encounters weaker corrective pressure.

## Problem pressure

The crucial observation was that bad franchise deviations often protect the franchise by failing. Audiences reject them, later creators ignore them, and their innovations do not become precedent. A great deviation has the opposite property: people want it retained.

The immediate case was franchise evolution around *Silent Hill*, especially the possibility that successful, acclaimed departures may change the category more effectively than obvious failures.

## Core model

```text
high-quality departure
        ↓
strong audience attachment
        ↓
low willingness to exclude
        ↓
new traits become legitimate precedent
        ↓
semantic dilution without aesthetic decline
```

## Claims and implications

Good dilution is more dangerous to strict category preservation precisely because it lacks the normal symptoms of failure. It can increase prestige, audience size, and artistic interest while decreasing specificity.

This family deliberately separates two judgments: **is the work good?** and **what does admitting this work do to the meaning of the franchise?**

## Examples

### Clean example

An acclaimed sequel differs substantially from the older franchise identity but is so compelling that its new structure becomes a model for later entries.

### Borderline example

A well-received installment introduces several new assumptions but also preserves much of the older grammar; the key dispute is whether the change is ordinary evolution or category drift.

### Cross-domain example

The same logic applies to institutions, political labels, genres, product categories, and intellectual traditions whose names persist while membership criteria change.

## Non-examples

A bad sequel that departs from identity but is ignored or repudiated is dilution, but not good dilution.

## Boundaries and failure modes

Calling something good dilution requires two independent arguments: that the work is genuinely strong by relevant evaluative standards, and that its inclusion broadens or weakens franchise identity. Neither follows automatically from the other.

“Dilution” here is semantic, not automatically qualitative. A broader category can contain better works than the narrower one it replaced.

## Operationalization / evidence

Track changes in category extension across time: which works are admitted, which traits cease to be necessary, what audiences expect from the label, what creators imitate, and whether formerly exceptional features become normal. Separate quality measures from identity measures.

## Relations

Good dilution **specializes** semantic dilution, **enables** quality-legitimized semantic drift, **overlaps with** non-self-correcting dilution, and tends to acquire canonical adhesion.

### Immediate context

The discussion distinguished ordinary franchise failure from the more subtle case where good work makes a broader or altered franchise definition harder to resist.

### Problem being solved

“Decline,” “bad sequel,” and “selling out” were inadequate because they assume aesthetic failure. The needed vocabulary had to describe identity change that can occur through success.
