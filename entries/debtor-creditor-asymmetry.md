# Debtor–creditor asymmetry

## Definition

**Debtor–creditor asymmetry** names one-sided individuation of fiscal subjects.

Before collection, the person is highly legible: identity, income, liability, deadlines, penalties, exact amount owed. After collection, the same institution may represent the contributor only as anonymous revenue and deny that contributor-specific downstream accounting is meaningful.

The asymmetry concerns which direction of obligation receives institutional precision.

## Problem pressure

The source crystallized the grievance:

> You’re visible as a debtor.

> You’re invisible as a creditor.

The state can prove that a person owes a precise amount, yet asking what happened to that amount downstream is treated as unreasonable.

## Core model

~~~text
citizen → state obligation
HIGH individuation / enforceability

state → citizen accountability
LOW individuation / weak standing
~~~

## Claims and implications

The term does not imply a taxpayer is legally a creditor in the ordinary debt-law sense. “Creditor” is an analytical analogy for residual claims.

The empirical question is how much asymmetry exists in specific systems. The normative question is whether compulsion justifies or aggravates it.

## Examples

### Clean example

A revenue agency can enforce a tiny underpayment while a taxpayer has no contributor-specific cause of action when an equally tiny attributable amount violates a statutory restriction.

### Borderline example

A state gives detailed individualized receipts but no enforceable downstream claim. Information symmetry improves while legal asymmetry remains.

## Non-examples

Different levels of detail arising from genuinely different technical tasks are not automatically this asymmetry. The concept does not require identical procedures on both sides.

## Boundaries and failure modes

The creditor metaphor can mislead if read as ordinary debt. Evidence should distinguish impossibility, administrative cost, legal design, and deliberate opacity.

## Operationalization / evidence

Compare granularity, enforceability, latency, error correction, and remedies when government asserts a fiscal claim against a person versus when a person asserts a residual claim against government.
