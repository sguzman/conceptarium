# Corporate-moral merger

## Definition

A **corporate-moral merger** occurs when commercial and moral incentive systems point in the same direction. The resulting decision cannot be neatly classified as either profit-seeking or ideological because each reinforces the other.

## Problem pressure

The term was needed to avoid false either/or explanations such as “the company only did this for money” versus “the employees imposed ideology.” Both can be true alongside prestige and reputational incentives.

The immediate problem was how abstract ideology, prestige, corporate incentives, and audience judgments can become concrete design choices without requiring a single centralized command.

## Core model

```text
market expansion
+ employee values
+ prestige rewards
+ reputational risk management
+ moral doctrine
        ↓ aligned incentives
same design choice becomes overdetermined
```

## Claims and implications

Merger can make institutional change durable because multiple subsystems reward the same outcome. It also makes motive attribution difficult from outside.

The entry is a mechanism for inquiry, not a presumption of motive. A specific application should identify which stages of the mechanism are evidenced and which remain hypothetical.

## Examples

### Clean example

A less sexualized design simultaneously appeals to a broader market, reduces reputational risk, fits employee values, and earns critical prestige.

### Borderline example

A design decision fits the predicted mechanism but could also be explained by story needs, technology, individual artist taste, market research, or production constraints.

### Cross-domain example

Comparable governance and allocation mechanisms can operate in advertising, fashion, film, publishing, social platforms, and institutional communication.

## Non-examples

A company grudgingly follows a regulation that conflicts with its market incentives and internal values.

## Boundaries and failure modes

Alignment should not be mistaken for coordination. Independent incentives can converge without conspiracy or centralized ideological control.

Avoid monocausal explanations. Corporate strategy, sincere moral beliefs, professional prestige, audience research, and personal aesthetics can align without being identical causes.

## Operationalization / evidence

Map the proposed causal chain stage by stage. Seek evidence for doctrine, status incentives, decision rules, allocation changes, and concrete output changes separately rather than inferring the whole pipeline from the final artifact.
